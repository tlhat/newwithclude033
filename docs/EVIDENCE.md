# Haneen: evidence base for the developmental support system

Scope: ages 18 months to 12 years. This is a learning and play app, not a diagnostic or clinical tool. It makes no diagnosis, no IQ or "brain age" claims, and no promise that practice in the app improves general intelligence. All data stays on the phone.

Every source below was opened (fetched) on 25 Nov 2026. Where a page only gave the abstract, the claim is limited to what the abstract says. Items marked UNVERIFIED are design choices that no source I opened directly supports.

## 2. What the evidence supports, by area

Strength scale: STRONG = meta-analysis or consensus of reviews. MODERATE = reviews with mixed or limited designs. WEAK = emerging, contested, or only inferred.

### 2.1 Screen time and the app's own limits
- Supported (S2): WHO says no sedentary screen time for 1-year-olds, no more than 1 hour for 2-year-olds, and no more than 1 hour for ages 3 to 4, "less is better". Reading and storytelling with a caregiver is encouraged when sitting. Sleep: 11 to 14 h at ages 1 to 2, 10 to 13 h at ages 3 to 4.
- Supported (S3): AAP 2016 recommends no more than 1 hour a day of high-quality media for ages 2 to 5, shared use with a parent, no screens during meals and for 1 hour before bed. Children under 2 learn mainly from hands-on exploration and caregivers, and have trouble transferring what they see on a screen to the real world. It also notes that responsive touchscreen apps and live video chat with a responsive adult can help 24-month-olds learn words.
- Strength: STRONG as guidance from health bodies, MODERATE as causal evidence.
- Limits: I found no age-specific cap for ages 6 to 12 in the pages I opened. Any cap for that band is a parent setting, not a research number (UNVERIFIED).
- App design: parent-set daily time limit with a built-in default of 20 min for 18 to 36 months used together with a parent, and at most 1 h for 3 to 5 (S2, S3). The 18 to 24 month band conflicts with WHO's "not recommended" for 1-year-olds, so for that band the app should default to off, require a parent to switch it on, and only offer short co-use activities (talking, naming, songs). Show a "time is up" screen with a real-world activity. Add a no-screens-before-bed reminder. The 20 min figure is a conservative design choice (UNVERIFIED).

### 2.2 Cognition: attention, memory, categories, patterns, logic
- Supported (S10): children learn more from apps that are active (minds-on, not just tapping), engaging, meaningful (linked to their life) and social, with a clear learning goal. Strength: MODERATE (review of learning-science literature, not a trial of any one app).
- Supported (S9): practice testing (asking the child to recall) and spaced practice rated high utility across ages and materials.
- App design: sorting, pattern, odd-one-out, memory-card and logic games, each with a clear goal and feedback, and a "now do it in real life" parent prompt.

### 2.3 Language and communication
- Supported (S11): dialogic reading (the adult asks questions, the child talks, the adult expands) raised expressive vocabulary more than normal reading: d = 0.59 over 9 studies (322 children), a moderate effect. The effect was much smaller for older children (4 and up). Strength: MODERATE, small samples.
- Supported (S3, S2): reading and storytelling with a caregiver is the recommended sedentary activity.
- App design: the app plays a word or short story and pauses with an open question ("what do you think happens next?") for the child to answer aloud to the parent. Mark spoken answers as parent-observed, not scored by the app. Vocabulary and listening games as before. No claim that the app itself teaches language to under-2s (S3).
- Limit: tying effects to Arabic specifically: I did not find an Arabic-language study in what I opened.

### 2.4 Social and emotional skills
- Supported (S12): school-based social and emotional learning programs (213 programs, 270,034 students, K to 12) improved social and emotional skills, behavior and academics, about an 11 percentile point gain in achievement. Recommended practices mattered. Strength: STRONG, but these are teacher-led school programs, not apps.
- Limit: transfer from an app to behavior is not shown by S12. Use the app to start a conversation and a real-world practice, not to claim behavior change.
- App design: emotion faces, "what would you do" stories, "please and thank you", sharing. Always end with a parent question.

### 2.5 Executive function (impulse control, flexibility, planning, working memory)
- Supported (S4): executive function (focus, holding information in mind, filtering distractions, switching) develops across childhood and responds to early experiences. Strength: STRONG as consensus, a policy-style overview.
- Supported (S5): many activity types improved executive function in ages 4 to 12 (computerized training, non-computer games, aerobics, martial arts, yoga, mindfulness, curricula). Successful programs involved repeated practice and gradually increasing challenge; children with weaker executive function benefited most; programs also addressing emotional, social and physical development may work better than narrow training. Strength: MODERATE (a review with mixed designs).
- Contested (S6, S7): brain-training programs reliably improve performance on the trained tasks, less on closely related tasks, and little evidence for distant tasks or everyday thinking. Working memory training meta-analysis: no reliable improvement on intelligence or other far-transfer measures. Strength of any "games make you smarter" claim: WEAK.
- App design: stop-and-go, rule switching, memory sequences and planning puzzles, with gradually harder levels. Label them as practice, never as "raises IQ" or "trains your brain". Pair with physical play and routines as real-world tasks.

### 2.6 Motor skills (eye-hand, touch control)
- Not sourced: I did not open a study on touchscreen motor skills. WHO (S2) supports at least 180 min a day of physical activity for ages 1 to 4, which a screen app cannot supply.
- App design (UNVERIFIED): tracing, dragging, tapping accuracy games are fine as practice and as a measure of in-app touch control only. Every session should prompt a real movement break.

### 2.7 Spaced review and mastery
- Supported (S8): a meta-analysis of 317 experiments found spaced learning beat massed learning, and the best gap grew with how long the child must remember. Strength: STRONG, but mostly verbal recall tasks in the lab.
- Supported (S9): practice testing and spaced practice are the two highest-utility techniques of ten reviewed.
- Limit: the evidence is mostly from older students and adults; the gaps for young children are not given in what I opened. The exact intervals (e.g. next day, 3 days, 7 days, 21 days) are a design choice (UNVERIFIED).
- App design: wrong answers come back later at growing gaps; "mastery" for a skill needs correct answers on separate days, not one lucky session.

### 2.8 Adaptive difficulty
- Supported (S5): successful programs progressively increased challenge. Strength: MODERATE.
- Limit: no source opened gave a specific rule (e.g. "move up after 3 right"). Treat the rule as a design choice and keep it simple and visible to the parent.

## 3. Skills map by age band

"Measure" is what the app records locally. It describes practice in the app, never ability, diagnosis, or IQ. Wording shown to parents: "practiced", "getting more confident", "needs more time".

### 18 to 36 months (parent co-use only; default screen time off or very short)
| Area | In-app activity | Measure | Parent real-world activity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Language | Name the picture, animal sounds, songs, hear-and-tap | Taps correct after hearing the word; parent confirms child said it | Name things at home and outside; read a picture book and ask "what is this?" |
| Cognition | Match the same, simple sorting by color or size | Correct matches, number of tries | Sort laundry, spoons, toys together |
| Social-emotional | Happy/sad faces, hello and goodbye | Parent marks "child pointed to the right face" | Name feelings during the day ("you look happy") |
| Executive | Wait-then-tap, 2-step "tap here then there" | Tapped after the cue; session length | Turn-taking games, songs with stop and go |
| Motor | Big targets, drag, tap, simple tracing | Touch accuracy only | Stacking blocks, scribbling, running, climbing |
Evidence: S2, S3 (screen limits and hands-on learning), S11 (talk and reading; I did not confirm the age range of its samples, strength MODERATE).

### 3 to 5 years
| Area | In-app activity | Measure | Parent real-world activity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Language | Letter sounds, missing letter, rhymes, story with a question pause | Correct answers; parent marks spoken answers | Dialogic reading: ask, listen, expand |
| Cognition | Patterns, odd-one-out, counting, shadow match, memory cards | Accuracy; spaced re-test result | Count stairs, find patterns on the street |
| Social-emotional | Feelings faces, "what would you do", sharing, please and thank you | Parent-marked conversation done | Talk about a recent real situation |
| Executive | Stop-and-go, rule switch (color then shape), short sequences | Accuracy at each level; level reached | Simon-says, clean-up routine, board games |
| Motor | Tracing letters, drag, simple mazes | Touch accuracy | Drawing, playdough, ball games |

### 6 to 8 years
| Area | In-app activity | Measure | Parent real-world activity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Language | Word building, reading short text, listening comprehension, story ordering | Accuracy; items due for spaced review | Read together, retell the story aloud |
| Cognition | Addition and subtraction, time, money, logic puzzles, memory | Accuracy; mastery stages (new, learning, confident, solid) across separate days | Shopping with money, cooking measures |

| Area | In-app activity | Measure | Parent real-world activity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Language | Longer text, vocabulary with context, spelling with spaced review | Accuracy; spaced review items | Book talk, writing a short paragraph |
| Cognition | Multi-step math, logic and strategy puzzles, pattern rules | Accuracy; spaced re-test | Plan a trip budget, family puzzles |

## 4. Confidence of mastery (how the app should report)
- A skill is "new" until it has a few answers, "learning" with mixed results, "confident" with correct answers on at least 2 separate days, "solid" after a spaced re-check days later still passes. The thresholds are a design choice (UNVERIFIED), based on the spacing idea in S8 and S9.
- Show confidence per activity, not per child.

## 5. Parent report rules
Show: time used, activities practiced, skills by stage, items due for review, and one real-world activity for the week. Do not show: scores compared to other children, percentiles, "IQ", "ability level", or any wording implying a diagnosis. Add a standing line: "This app is for practice and play. It does not diagnose. If you are worried about your child's development, talk to a doctor or specialist." (Design choice.)

## 6. Evidence badge for each activity
Each activity record carries: skill area, age band, evidence IDs from section 1, strength (STRONG / MODERATE / WEAK / UNVERIFIED), and a one-line honest limit. Activities with only UNVERIFIED support are shown as "play" in the parent view, not as "skill training".

## 7. Claims the app and the store listing must not make
- "Makes your child smarter", "raises IQ", "trains the brain", or "ready for school in X weeks". Reason: S6, S7.
- That the app replaces reading, play, sleep, movement or time with people. Reason: S2, S3.
- Diagnosis, screening or risk labels of any kind.
- That any single activity here is clinically validated. None has been tested in Arabic-speaking children by the sources I opened.

## 8. Open items for the owner
1. Check the revised AAP statement (https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2025-075320) before publishing numbers.
2. Decide the default screen limit per band (suggested above, a design choice).
3. If wider claims are wanted later, get a child-development specialist to review.
